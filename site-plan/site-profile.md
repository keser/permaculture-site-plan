## Location & Climate Classification

- **Region:** a 0.43-acre residential lot in the Portland, CT area (~41.6°N).
- **Köppen classification:** hot-summer continental (Dfa) — four distinct seasons: cold,
  potentially snowy winters and hot, humid summers.
- **Design implication:** this is a genuine four-season climate, not a mild coastal one — plant
  and structure choices need to handle real winter cold/snow load *and* real summer heat/humidity,
  not just one or the other.

## Sun / Solar

Solar noon altitude swings roughly 47° between solstices at this latitude — near-overhead in
summer, low and skimming the southern sky in winter — with about 6 hours more daylight at the
summer solstice than the winter one.

**Design implication:** the low winter sun travels a short, low arc through the southern sky, so
anything on the south side of a bed — a structure, an evergreen, a neighbor's roofline — casts a
long winter shadow. Any spot counted on for winter solar gain needs a genuinely clear southern
sightline, not just "faces south." Two concrete ties to the master plan:
- **R3** (limbing lower branches on the pines and oaks) raises the effective canopy line, letting
  more low-angle winter sun reach ground level beneath those trees.
- **R17 / D1 / D3 / D4** (raised beds and new plantings): a spot that reads as full sun in July
  can still be shaded by a neighbor's roofline or a deciduous tree (leafed out, or even bare in
  winter) at the low winter sun angle — worth an on-the-ground shadow check near the December
  solstice specifically, not just judging placement off current summer canopy.

**Not yet done:** this is sun-*angle* analysis, not a rendered shadow study. Actual shade-casting
geometry from specific trees/structures/neighboring rooflines hasn't been modeled — worth doing
once structure placement (shed, beds) firms up enough to be worth the effort.

## Wind

Mid-latitude prevailing westerlies with a clear seasonal split: **northwest-to-north winds
dominate the colder months**, while **southwest-to-south winds predominate roughly April through
September**. "Mainly/usually breezy" is the most common condition in every season; strong wind
events are rare.

**Design implication:** the NW quadrant is the exposed side for winter wind — prioritize
windbreak density there before mini-split/heating-load decisions (ties to **C4**, the electrical
panel upgrade that will carry that load), and factor it into where the pollard willow row (**S5**)
gets planted if it's meant to double as a windbreak. Summer's SW airflow is also the direction the
regional ozone problem arrives from (see Pollutants, below) — the two sectors compound on that
side of the property.

## Fire

Local wildfire risk is low relative to the western US — elevated humidity keeps fuel moisture
stable most of the year. Risk isn't zero (dry autumn stretches, and regional smoke transport from
Canadian wildfires has occurred), but doesn't justify aggressive defensible-space design.

**Design implication:** existing clearing tasks (**R2** deadwood/branch fall, **R19** wood pile
reorganization) already reduce accumulated dry fuel near structures — the practical, low-effort
version of fire-sector design at this risk level.

## Pollutants

The county carries an **F grade for ozone**, averaging roughly 7 unhealthy ozone days per year,
regionally trending toward "severe" nonattainment. This is a transport problem, not a local
point-source one — precursor pollutants arrive predominantly on the same summer SW wind identified
above. No known active local point-source pollution on or near the site.

**Design implication:** dense planting on the SW-facing edge (trees/shrubs) provides some real
particulate/ozone-precursor filtering — a reasonable secondary justification (not the primary one)
for native tree diversity in **D1**. Practically: schedule strenuous outdoor work for mornings on
summer high-ozone days rather than midafternoon peak.

## Crime

The area ranks well above average for safety regionally.

**Design implication:** essentially none needed — this sector doesn't justify security-driven
design choices. Fence and layout decisions (**R16** garden fence removal, **R17** raised bed
fencing) can be driven entirely by function (deer, aesthetics) rather than security.

## Ice

The Northeast sees a major, damaging ice storm roughly every 15–25 years; even 1/4"–1/2" of
accretion is enough to snap small branches and weak limbs.

**Design implication:** direct reinforcement of two Phase 1 tasks — **R3** (limbing lower branches
on pines/oaks) and **R8** (cutting back the maples) aren't just about tick habitat and reuse value,
they also reduce weak-limb load that snaps first in an ice event. Prioritize weak, low-angle, or
overextended limbs over walkways and the shed (**C5**) site first.

## Wildlife Corridors

The site sits close to a significant regional river corridor — documented stopover/breeding
habitat for migratory songbirds and waterfowl. Deer are locally confirmed in the area (a 2017
regional deer disease event began nearby, with 50+ symptomatic deer documented) — direct evidence
of active local deer pressure.

**Design implication:** genuinely favorable for the property's habitat goals (**D1** native
trees/shrubs, **D5** chicken/evergreen habitat cleanup) — the site can meaningfully contribute to
a real regional bird/pollinator corridor. It's also the practical reason to expect real deer browse
pressure on new plantings: raised beds (**R17**) and new perennials/shrubs (**D1, D3, D4**) should
assume deer will test them from the start, consistent with the phased-planting approach already
preferred for this project.

## Passive Design & Structure Implications

From a bioclimatic study run against this climate data — most directly relevant to **DS1** (shed
& storage design) and any future structure planning.

**Overall:** top architectural response to this climate is "provide shelter from the cold" (over
"take the inside outside"). Solar gain on the sunniest orientation is "quite significant" with
"high" importance of solar control/shading. Most impactful single intervention for making outdoor
space usable is a windbreak, followed by sun shade — combining the two moves outdoor-usable-time
from 13% to a meaningfully higher share of the year.

**Facade shading & glazing by orientation:**

| Orientation | Max glazing (typical envelope) | Max glazing (best-practice envelope) | Shading recommendation | Best shading option |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| North | 41% | 73% | Not needed | — |
| Northeast | 41% | 73% | Not needed | — |
| East | 20% | 73% | Consider shading; don't block beneficial warming sun | Brise soleil |
| Southeast | 20% | 73% | Consider shading; don't block beneficial warming sun | Eggcrate |
| South | 20% | 73% | Recommended; don't block beneficial warming sun | Brise soleil |
| Southwest | 20% | 73% | Recommended | Eggcrate |
| West | 20% | 73% | Recommended | Automated blinds |
| Northwest | 20% | 73% | Recommended | Automated blinds |

**Design implication:** if the new shed (**C5**/**DS1**) has any windows or glazed openings,
north/northeast-facing glazing can be generous with no shading needed; anything east through
northwest benefits from shading, with south specifically needing shading that still admits
low-angle winter sun rather than blocking it outright.
